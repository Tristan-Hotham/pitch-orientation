The usual advice is to lay a cricket pitch north to south, or the evening sun will dazzle the batter. I measured every cricket pitch in OpenStreetMap, and every sports pitch in the UK, to see who follows it. Cricket does. Tennis does. Football does not care.

Cricket is a game of long summer evenings, and long summer evenings end with the sun low in the west. Stand at the crease of a pitch that runs east to west and, for an hour or two, the bowler comes at you out of the glare. So design guides advise running the pitch roughly north to south; the Western Australian government's sports dimensions guide, for one, says so for cricket pitches and tennis courts alike. FIFA's stadium guidelines say big football stadiums mostly run north to south too, but the ordinary park pitch gets little advice.

OpenStreetMap makes the rule testable. Volunteers have traced {{n.world_cricket|int}} cricket pitches around the world and {{n.uk_polygons|int}} sports pitches of every kind in the UK. For each one I found the smallest rectangle that fits round its outline and measured how far its long side turns from the north to south line.

Three things called a pitch

The first job was to work out what a "cricket pitch" on the map actually is. Mappers use the word for three different things. The strip is the 22 yards between the wickets, a thin rectangle about {{n.strip_median_length|f1}} m by {{n.strip_median_width|f1}} m as mapped, and its long side is the line of play. The square is the block of a dozen strips side by side in the middle of a club ground, and its strips could run either way across it. The ground is the whole field, usually round, with no direction at all. I sorted them by size and shape: {{n.cricket_strip|int}} strips, {{n.cricket_square|int}} squares, {{n.cricket_ground|int}} grounds. Then I looked at the strips, drawn with the roads and buildings around them, and found that about one in twelve were really banks of practice nets at the edge of a ground, which I moved to their own pile.

The rule holds

Of the {{n.cricket_strip|int}} strips, {{strips_world.ns20|pc}} lie within 20 degrees of north to south. If pitches pointed at random, only {{chance20|pc}} would. Just {{strips_world.ew20|pc}} run within 20 degrees of east to west, the direction the advice exists to avoid. In Australia, the country with the most mapped strips, the share is {{by_country.Australia.strip.ns20|pc}}. In the UK it is {{uk.strip.ns20|pc}}, and in New Zealand only {{by_country.New Zealand.strip.ns20|pc}}.

{{IMG|b_map.png|Three maps on parchment, of Britain, south-east Australia and New Zealand, with every cricket strip as a short coloured line. Most Australian lines are red and upright; most British lines too, with some blue ones lying across; New Zealand's point more ways|Every mapped strip in the three countries with the most, tinted by how far it turns from north to south.}}

British mappers rarely draw single strips, but they often draw the square, and the squares agree. A square is usually wider across its strips than along them, and {{uk.square.ew20|pc}} of the {{uk.square.n|int}} UK squares have their long side running east to west, against {{uk.square.ns20|pc}} running north to south. A row of strips side by side across an east to west block is a row of strips running north to south.

Tennis yes, football no

Tennis is the only other sport that keeps the rule. Of {{uk.tennis.n|int}} UK tennis courts, {{uk.tennis.ns20|pc}} lie within 20 degrees of north to south and only {{uk.tennis.ew20|pc}} within 20 degrees of east to west. Football is a different story: {{uk.football.ns20|pc}} of {{uk.football.n|int}} pitches run north to south and {{uk.football.ew20|pc}} east to west. Rugby is the same. These pitches follow the shape of the field they were squeezed into, and a rectangular field is as likely to lie one way as the other.

How much does the sun matter?

To see what the rule protects against, I modelled the sun: for each latitude and pitch direction, the minutes in a season when the sun is less than {{glare.alt_max|int}} degrees high and within {{glare.half|int}} degrees of the line of the pitch, from ten in the morning until sunset. In an English season, a pitch running east to west gets about {{glare.uk_lat52.glare_ew_h|int}} hours of that low sun down its length. A north to south pitch gets none. But so does anything from {{glare.uk_lat52.zero_lo|raw}} to {{glare.uk_lat52.zero_hi|raw}} degrees from north: the safe arc is wide, and it leans towards the north-east, because the midsummer evening sun sets in the north-west. The rule as builders keep it is stricter than the sun demands.

The street wins, except for cricket

For every strip and square, and thousands of other pitches, I read the roads within {{streets_sample.radius_m|int}} m from OpenFreeMap's vector tiles and found the main direction of the local street grid. In built-up places where the grid runs well off the compass, most pitches simply line up with the streets: {{streets.football.urban_closer_to_grid|pc}} of football pitches, {{streets.tennis.urban_closer_to_grid|pc}} of tennis courts, {{streets.rugby.urban_closer_to_grid|pc}} of rugby pitches. Cricket strips do so least often, {{streets.strip.urban_closer_to_grid|pc}}, and in Australia only {{streets.strip_aus.urban_closer_to_grid|pc}}. Of the sports I measured, cricket is the least likely to follow the streets, which fits a game that tries to keep the sun out of the batter's eyes.

What the map cannot say

OpenStreetMap is uneven. Strips are mapped mostly in Australia, New Zealand and Britain, and hardly at all in India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh or the Caribbean, where most of the world's cricket is played, and the great Test grounds are usually mapped as stadiums rather than pitches. So the comparison between hemispheres and latitudes rests on a few countries. My size rules will let the odd single-lane net through as a strip. And the sun model is a sketch: it ignores trees, stands, cloud and the real hours of matches.

How I did this

The pitch outlines come from OpenStreetMap, extracted with the QLever query engine. Each outline was laid flat around its own centre with north kept true, and the long side of the smallest rectangle that fits round it gives its direction. Pitches of other sports were kept only when the rectangle has the size and shape of one playing area, so that rows of tennis courts side by side, whose long side runs across the courts, are left out. The street grid comes from OpenFreeMap's vector tiles. The sun's position uses Spencer's 1971 formula and Bennett's refraction. All the code and data are on GitHub, and the map lets you look at any pitch you like.
